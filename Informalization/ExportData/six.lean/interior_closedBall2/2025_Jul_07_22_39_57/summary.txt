We will show that the interior of the closed ball centered at \( x \) with radius \( r \) coincides exactly with the open ball centered at \( x \) with the same radius. First, if \( r < 0 \), by the definition of metric balls, both the closed and open balls with negative radius are empty sets; hence their interiors are empty as well, establishing the equality trivially in this case. Next, for nonnegative \( r \), it suffices to prove that the interior of the closed ball is contained within the open ball, since the reverse inclusion is immediate by the openness of the open ball. Assume a point \( y \) lies in the interior of the closed ball centered at \( x \) with radius \( r \). We consider whether the distance \( d(y,x) \) is less than or equal to \( r \). If \( d(y,x) < r \), then \( y \) is clearly in the open ball, completing the argument for this case. For the case \( d(y,x) = r \), we define a function \( f: \mathbb{R} \to E \) by \( f(c) = x + c(y - x) \), which is continuous as it is composed of scalar multiplication and addition. The closed ball centered at \( x \) with radius \( d(y,x) \) corresponds under \( f \) to the interval \([-1,1]\). Since \( f(1) = y \) and because \( y \) lies in the interior of the closed ball, the point \( 1 \) is an interior point of \([-1,1]\). By continuity, the interior of the preimage contains \( 1 \), so \( y \) lies in the interior of the closed ball, which is precisely the open ball centered at \( x \) with radius \( d(y,x) \). Finally, by examining the inequalities that characterize membership in the interval \([-1,1]\), and using properties of norms and distances, we confirm that any point in the preimage must satisfy \( |c| \leq 1 \), thereby concluding that \( y \) lies in the open ball. Thus, the interior of the closed ball equals the open ball with the same center and radius.
