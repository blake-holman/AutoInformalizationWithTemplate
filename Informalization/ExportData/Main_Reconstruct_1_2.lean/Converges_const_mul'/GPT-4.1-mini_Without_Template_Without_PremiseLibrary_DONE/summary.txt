We will show that if a sequence \( (x_n) \) converges to a limit \( a \), then the sequence \( (\alpha x_n) \), obtained by multiplying each term by a constant \(\alpha\), converges to \(\alpha a\). To this end, let \(\varepsilon > 0\) be given. Since \(x_n \to a\), there exists a natural number \(n_0\) such that for all \(n \geq n_0\), the inequality 
\[
|x_n - a| < \frac{\varepsilon}{|\alpha| + 1}
\]
holds. This choice is valid because the denominator \( |\alpha| + 1 \) is strictly positive. Fix such an \(n_0\). Now, for any \(n \geq n_0\), consider the distance between \(\alpha x_n\) and \(\alpha a\):
\[
|\alpha x_n - \alpha a| = |\alpha| \cdot |x_n - a|.
\]
If \(|\alpha| > 0\), multiplying the previous inequality by \(|\alpha|\) yields
\[
|\alpha| \cdot |x_n - a| < |\alpha| \cdot \frac{\varepsilon}{|\alpha| + 1} < \varepsilon,
\]
since \(|\alpha| < |\alpha| + 1\). On the other hand, if \(\alpha = 0\), the expression simplifies to zero which trivially satisfies the inequality. Thus, for all \(n \geq n_0\), we have 
\[
|\alpha x_n - \alpha a| < \varepsilon,
\]
which by definition establishes the convergence of \(\alpha x_n\) to \(\alpha a\). This completes the proof.
