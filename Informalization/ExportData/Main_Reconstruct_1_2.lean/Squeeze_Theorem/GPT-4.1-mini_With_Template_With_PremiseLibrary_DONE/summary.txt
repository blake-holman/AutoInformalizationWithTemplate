We will show that if a sequence \( (z_n) \) is bounded above and below by two sequences \( (x_n) \) and \( (y_n) \), both converging to the same limit \( a \), then the sequence \( (z_n) \) also converges to \( a \). Let \( \varepsilon > 0 \) be given. Since \( (x_n) \) converges to \( a \), there exists a natural number \( n_1 \) such that for all \( n \geq n_1 \), we have \( |x_n - a| < \varepsilon \). Similarly, because \( (y_n) \) converges to \( a \), there exists \( n_2 \) such that for all \( n \geq n_2 \), \( |y_n - a| < \varepsilon \). Define \( n_0 = \max\{n_1, n_2\} \); then for every \( n \geq n_0 \), both \( |x_n - a| < \varepsilon \) and \( |y_n - a| < \varepsilon \) hold. By the definition of absolute value inequalities, this implies the chain of inequalities \( a - \varepsilon < x_n \leq z_n \leq y_n < a + \varepsilon \). Hence, \( z_n \) lies between \( a - \varepsilon \) and \( a + \varepsilon \), which means \( |z_n - a| < \varepsilon \) for all \( n \geq n_0 \). Since \( \varepsilon \) was arbitrary, it follows that \( (z_n) \) converges to \( a \), completing the proof.
