We will show that if for each natural number \( n \), the value \( z(n) \) lies between \( x(n) \) and \( y(n) \), and if both sequences \( x \) and \( y \) converge to the same limit \( a \), then \( z \) also converges to \( a \). To this end, let \( \varepsilon > 0 \) be arbitrary. Since \( x \) converges to \( a \), there exists \( n_1 \in \mathbb{N} \) such that for all \( n \geq n_1 \), \( |x_n - a| < \varepsilon \). Similarly, because \( y \) converges to \( a \), there exists \( n_2 \in \mathbb{N} \) such that for all \( n \geq n_2 \), \( |y_n - a| < \varepsilon \). Define \( n_0 = \max(n_1, n_2) \). Then for all \( n \geq n_0 \), we have both \( |x_n - a| < \varepsilon \) and \( |y_n - a| < \varepsilon \), which imply \( a - \varepsilon < x_n \leq z_n \leq y_n < a + \varepsilon \), since \( z_n \) is squeezed between \( x_n \) and \( y_n \) by assumption. Consequently, the distance between \( z_n \) and \( a \) satisfies \( |z_n - a| < \varepsilon \) for all \( n \geq n_0 \). As \( \varepsilon \) was arbitrary, it follows that the sequence \( z \) converges to \( a \), completing the proof.
