We will show that if the set \( S \) is nonempty, then it must contain a minimal element, that is, an element \( x \in S \) such that \( x \leq y \) for every \( y \in S \). Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that no element in \( S \) is minimal; equivalently, for every element in \( S \), there exists another element in \( S \) that is strictly smaller. To analyze this, we employ strong induction on the natural numbers to demonstrate that no natural number belongs to \( S \). Assume an arbitrary natural number \( n \) and, as the induction hypothesis, suppose that all natural numbers less than \( n \) are not in \( S \). Now, if \( n \in S \), then by our assumption, there must be some \( y \in S \) with \( y < n \). However, by the induction hypothesis, \( y \notin S \), leading to a contradiction. Thus, \( n \notin S \), and since \( n \) was arbitrary, it follows that \( S \) contains no natural numbers. On the other hand, since \( S \) is assumed nonempty, there exists an element \( x \in S \). Applying the result, \( x \) cannot be a natural number, but at the same time it must be in \( S \), giving a contradiction. This inconsistency shows that our initial assumption is false, and therefore \( S \) must contain at least one minimal element.
