We will show that if the set \( S \) is nonempty, then it contains a minimal element; that is, there exists an element \( x \in S \) such that for every \( y \in S \), \( x \leq y \). Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that no such minimal element exists. This means that for every element \( x \in S \), there is another element \( y \in S \) with \( y < x \). We proceed to prove that no natural number belongs to \( S \) by strong induction on the natural numbers. Assume that some natural number \( n \) is in \( S \). By the property of \( S \), there exists \( y \in S \) with \( y < n \). However, by the induction hypothesis, all natural numbers less than \( n \) are not in \( S \), so \( y \notin S \), a contradiction. Hence, no natural number belongs to \( S \). Returning to the assumption that \( S \) is nonempty, there exists some element \( x \in S \), but since \( x \) cannot be a natural number, it contradicts the conclusion that no natural number is in \( S \). Thus, the assumption that no minimal element exists leads to a contradiction, and it follows that \( S \) contains a minimal element. This completes the proof.
