We will show that for any positive real numbers \(a\) and \(b\), there exists a natural number \(n\) such that \((n+1) \cdot a > b\). Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that for every natural number \(n\), \((n+1) \cdot a \leq b\). Define the set \( C = \{ (k+1) \cdot a \mid k \in \mathbb{N} \} \). This set is nonempty since, for example, \(a = (0+1) \cdot a \in C\), and it is bounded above by \(b\) because, by assumption, every element of \(C\) is at most \(b\). By the completeness property of the real numbers, \(C\) has a supremum \(x\). Since, for any natural number \(n\), \((n+2) \cdot a = ((n+1) + 1) \cdot a\) belongs to \(C\), it follows that \((n+2) \cdot a \leq x\). Rearranging this inequality yields \((n+1) \cdot a \leq x - a\), thus showing that \(x - a\) is an upper bound of \(C\). By the minimality of the supremum \(x\), we must have \(x \leq x - a\). However, this is impossible since \(a > 0\). This contradiction arises from our initial assumption, so we conclude that there exists a natural number \(n\) such that \((n+1) \cdot a > b\), completing the proof.
