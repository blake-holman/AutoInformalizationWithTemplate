We will show that for positive real numbers \( a > 0 \) and \( b > 0 \), there exists a natural number \( n \) such that \((n + 1) \cdot a > b\). Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that for every natural number \( n \), the inequality \((n + 1) \cdot a \leq b\) holds. Define the set \( C \) as all real numbers \( s \) for which there exists a natural number \( k \) such that \( s = (k + 1) \cdot a \). The set \( C \) is nonempty since \( a = (0 + 1) \cdot a \) is an element of \( C \). Moreover, \( b \) is an upper bound of \( C \) because by assumption, every element of the form \((k + 1) \cdot a \) is less than or equal to \( b \).

Since \( C \) is nonempty and bounded above, it has a supremum \( x \). For any natural number \( n \), consider the element \((n + 2) \cdot a \in C\). By the definition of the least upper bound, \((n + 2) \cdot a \leq x\). Using this inequality and the properties of arithmetic, it follows that \((n + 1) \cdot a \leq x - a\) for every natural number \( n \). Thus, \( x - a \) is also an upper bound of \( C \). But since \( x \) is defined as the least upper bound, we must have \( x \leq x - a \), which contradicts the positivity of \( a \). This contradiction shows that our initial assumption is false, and therefore, there exists some natural number \( n \) such that \((n + 1) \cdot a > b\).
