We will show that for positive real numbers \( a \) and \( b \), there exists a natural number \( n \) such that \((n + 1) \cdot a > b\). Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that for every natural number \( n \), \((n + 1) \cdot a \leq b\). Define the set \( C = \{ (k + 1) \cdot a \mid k \in \mathbb{N} \} \). This set is nonempty since \( a = (0 + 1) \cdot a \in C \), and is bounded above by \( b \) due to our initial assumption. By the completeness property of the real numbers, \( C \) has a least upper bound, say \( x = \sup C \). For every natural number \( n \), note that \((n + 2) \cdot a = ((n + 1) + 1) \cdot a \in C\), so \((n + 2) \cdot a \leq x\). Rearranging this inequality, we obtain \((n + 1) \cdot a \leq x - a\). Since this holds for arbitrary elements of \( C \), it follows that \( x - a \) is also an upper bound for \( C \). However, by the definition of supremum as the least upper bound, we must have \( x \leq x - a \), which is impossible because \( a > 0 \). This contradiction shows that our initial assumption was false, and therefore there exists a natural number \( n \) such that \((n + 1) \cdot a > b\).
