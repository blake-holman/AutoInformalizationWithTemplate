We will show that for every positive real number \( a \) and for any positive real number \( b \), there exists a natural number \( n \) such that \((n + 1) \cdot a > b\). Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that for every natural number \( n \), the inequality \((n + 1) \cdot a \leq b\) holds. Define the set 
\[
C = \{ s \in \mathbb{R} : s = (k + 1) \cdot a \text{ for some } k \in \mathbb{N} \}.
\] 
We observe that \( C \) is non-empty since \( a = (0 + 1) \cdot a \in C \). By our assumption, every element of \( C \) satisfies \( s \leq b \), so \( b \) is an upper bound of \( C \). Since \( C \) is non-empty and bounded above, by the completeness property of the real numbers, \( C \) has a supremum, which we denote by \( x \).

Next, we show that for every natural number \( n \), the quantity \((n + 2) \cdot a\) also lies in \( C \) and thus satisfies \((n + 2) \cdot a \leq x\). From this, it follows that \((n + 1) \cdot a + a \leq x\), so \((n + 1) \cdot a \leq x - a\). Since this holds for all \( n \), \( x - a \) is also an upper bound of \( C \). By the definition of supremum, \( x \leq x - a \).

However, this inequality contradicts the fact that \( a > 0 \) because it would imply \( 0 \leq -a \), which is impossible. This contradiction arises from the initial assumption that no natural number \( n \) satisfies \((n + 1) \cdot a > b\). Hence, we conclude that such an \( n \) must exist, completing the proof.
