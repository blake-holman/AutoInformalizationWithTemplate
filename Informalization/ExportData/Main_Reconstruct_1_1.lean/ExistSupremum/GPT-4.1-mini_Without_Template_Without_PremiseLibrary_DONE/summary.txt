We will prove that every nonempty set \( C \subseteq \mathbb{R} \) that is bounded above has a supremum. To this end, define \( B \) as the set of all upper bounds of \( C \); since \( C \) is bounded above, \( B \) is nonempty. Next, let \( A = \mathbb{R} \setminus B \) be the complement of \( B \). We show that \( (A, B) \) forms a Dedekind cut by verifying that \( A \cap B = \emptyset \), \( A \cup B = \mathbb{R} \), \( A \) is nonempty (as one can find elements less than members of \( C \) not serving as upper bounds), and that every element of \( A \) is strictly less than every element of \( B \), an order relation established via contradiction. Since \( (A, B) \) is a Dedekind cut, either \( A \) has a greatest element or \( B \) has a least element. In the case where \( B \) has a least element \( x \), \( x \) serves as the least upper bound of \( C \). Otherwise, assume \( A \) has a greatest element \( x \). Because \( x \in A \), it is not an upper bound of \( C \), so there exists \( c \in C \) with \( x < c \). The interval between \( x \) and \( c \) is nonempty; taking any \( z \) in this interval shows \( z \in A \) with \( z > x \), contradicting that \( x \) is the greatest element of \( A \). This contradiction confirms that \( B \) must have a least element \( x \), which is the supremum of \( C \). Therefore, the supremum of \( C \) exists, completing the proof.
