We will show that every nonempty set \( C \) of real numbers that is bounded above has a supremum. Assume \( C \) is nonempty and bounded above, and let \( B \) denote the set of all upper bounds of \( C \). Since \( C \) is bounded above, \( B \) is nonempty. Define \( A \) as the complement of \( B \) in the real numbers. We claim that \( (A, B) \) forms a Dedekind cut by verifying the required properties: \( A \) and \( B \) are nonempty, disjoint, their union is the real numbers, every element of \( A \) is less than every element of \( B \), and \( A \) contains no greatest element.

To see that \( A \) is nonempty, let \( c \in C \) and note that \( c - 1 < c \) implies \( c - 1 \in A \), since elements less than \( c \) cannot be upper bounds of \( C \). Thus, \( A \neq \emptyset \). Next, we show \( A \cap B = \emptyset \) since \( A \) is defined as the complement of \( B \). Also, \( A \cup B \) equals the entire set of real numbers by definition.

We now prove that for any \( a \in A \) and \( b \in B \), \( a < b \). Suppose to the contrary that there exist \( a \in A \) and \( b \in B \) with \( a \geq b \). Since \( b \) is an upper bound, \( a \) would also be an upper bound, implying \( a \in B \), which contradicts \( a \in A \). Thus, \( a < b \) holds for all such \( a \) and \( b \).

By the completeness property characterized by Dedekind cuts, either \( A \) has a maximum element or \( B \) has a minimum element. If \( B \) has a minimum element \( x \), then \( x \) is the least upper bound (supremum) of \( C \). Alternatively, if \( A \) has a maximum element \( x \), we show \( x \) is also the supremum of \( C \). Since \( x \in A \), it is not an upper bound of \( C \), so there exists \( c \in C \) with \( x < c \). The interval \( (x, c) \) is nonempty (for example, containing the midpoint \( \frac{x + c}{2} \)) and any \( z \) in that interval must belong to \( A \), contradicting the maximality of \( x \) in \( A \). This contradiction implies \( A \) cannot have a maximum, and the supremum must be given by the minimum element of \( B \).

Therefore, there exists a real number \( a \), namely the minimum element of \( B \), which serves as the supremum of the set \( C \). This completes the proof.
