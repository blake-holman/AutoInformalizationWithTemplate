Assume that \(\varepsilon\) is a positive real number. We will show that there exists a natural number \(n\) such that \(\frac{1}{n+1} < \varepsilon\). By the Archimedean principle, since \(\varepsilon > 0\) and \(1 > 0\), there exists a natural number \(n\) such that \((n+1)\varepsilon > 1\). Fix such an \(n\). To prove the desired inequality, it suffices to show that \(\frac{1}{n+1} < \varepsilon\). First, note that \(n + 1 > 0\) because \(1 > 0\) and \(n\) is a natural number, so the sum is positive. Using the fact that for a positive divisor, an inequality involving division is equivalent to an inequality involving multiplication, \(\frac{1}{n+1} < \varepsilon\) holds if and only if \(1 < (n+1)\varepsilon\). By commutativity of multiplication, this is equivalent to \(1 < \varepsilon (n+1)\), which we established by choice of \(n\). Therefore, such an \(n\) exists, completing the proof.
