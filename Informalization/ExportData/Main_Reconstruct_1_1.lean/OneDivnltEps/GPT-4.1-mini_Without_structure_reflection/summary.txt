We will show that for every positive real number \(\varepsilon\), there exists a natural number \(n\) such that the reciprocal of \(n + 1\) is less than \(\varepsilon\). By the Archimedean property, since \(\varepsilon > 0\), there exists a natural number \(n\) such that \((n + 1) \times \varepsilon > 1\). Note that \(1 > 0\) is clearly true. We fix such an \(n\) and observe that to prove the reciprocal inequality, it suffices to show that \(\frac{1}{n + 1} < \varepsilon\). Since \(n\) is a natural number, \(n + 1 > 0\), which follows from the fact that \(0 < 1\). Because division by a positive number preserves inequalities, proving \(\frac{1}{n + 1} < \varepsilon\) is equivalent to proving \(1 < \varepsilon \cdot (n + 1)\). Using the commutativity of multiplication, this reduces to \(1 < (n + 1) \times \varepsilon\), which holds by our initial choice of \(n\). Thus, the desired inequality is established, completing the proof.
