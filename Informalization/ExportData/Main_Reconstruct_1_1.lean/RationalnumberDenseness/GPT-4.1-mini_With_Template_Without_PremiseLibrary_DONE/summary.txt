We will show that for every real number \( x \) and every positive real number \( \varepsilon \), there exists a rational number \( q \) such that the distance between \( x \) and \( q \) is less than \( \varepsilon \). We proceed by case analysis according to the sign of \( x \).

First, consider the case when \( x > 0 \). Since \(\varepsilon > 0\), by the Archimedean property, there exists a natural number \( n \) such that \(\frac{1}{n+1} < \varepsilon\). Again by the Archimedean property, there exists a natural number \( m \) with \(\frac{m+1}{n+1} > x\). Define the set \( M = \{ m \in \mathbb{N} \mid \frac{m+1}{n+1} > x \} \), which is nonempty by construction, and let \( m \) be its minimal element. Set \( q = \frac{m+1}{n+1} \), a rational number exceeding \( x \).

To show that \( |x - q| < \varepsilon \), note \( |x - q| = q - x \) since \( q > x \). It suffices to prove \( q - x < \varepsilon \). Given \(\frac{1}{n+1} < \varepsilon\), it is enough to establish \( q - x \leq \frac{1}{n+1} \). Rewriting, this inequality becomes \( q - \frac{1}{n+1} \leq x \). Substituting \( q = \frac{m+1}{n+1} \), we get \( \frac{m}{n+1} \leq x \).

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that \( \frac{m}{n+1} > x \). If \( m \geq 1 \), then \( m - 1 \) is a natural number and \(\frac{(m-1)+1}{n+1} = \frac{m}{n+1} > x\), implying \( m-1 \in M \), contradicting the minimality of \( m \). If \( m = 0 \), then \( \frac{m}{n+1} = 0 > x \), contradicting the positivity of \( x \). Therefore, the assumption is false, and \( \frac{m}{n+1} \leq x \) holds. Consequently, \( q - x \leq \frac{1}{n+1} < \varepsilon \), completing the case for \( x > 0 \).

Next, consider the case \( x = 0 \). Choosing \( q = 0 \) clearly satisfies \( |0 - 0| = 0 < \varepsilon \), fulfilling the requirement trivially.

Finally, when \( x < 0 \), observe that \( -x > 0 \). By the previous argument, there is a rational number \( q \) such that \( | -x - q | < \varepsilon \). Then, \( |-x - q| = |x + (-q)| \), so by setting \( q' = -q \), a rational number since rational numbers are closed under negation, we have \( |x - q'| < \varepsilon \), completing the case for negative \( x \).

Thus, in all cases, there exists a rational number \( q \) approximating \( x \) within any positive distance \( \varepsilon \), proving the density of the rationals in the real numbers.
